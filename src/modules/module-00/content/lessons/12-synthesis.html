Module Synthesis

The whole module in one sentenceYour finances are a system of stocks and flows. Time turns flows into stocks through compounding. Inflation, taxes and expensive debt drain that conversion. Liquidity and insurance protect it from shocks, and fraud attacks it directly.

What you now understand

  • {{i:check}} Structure. A household has flows (income, spending, saving) and stocks (assets, liabilities, net worth). The cash-flow statement is the video; the balance sheet is the photograph.
  • {{i:check}} Time. Money compounds, so time matters more than rate, and future money must be discounted to be compared with money today.
  • {{i:check}} Purchasing power. Returns that matter are real (after inflation) and after tax. A "safe" deposit can lose purchasing power.
  • {{i:check}} Plans. Goals are future costs in future rupees; the start date is the strongest lever; the horizon decides how much volatility you can accept.
  • {{i:check}} Buffers. Liquidity buys time; insurance removes ruinous outcomes. Both cost money on average, and both are worth it for the right risks.
  • {{i:check}} Threats. Expensive debt is compounding against you; tax drag compounds too; fraud offers returns without risk, which do not exist.

Key relationships

Spending grows faster than income↓Savings rate falls every year
Leverage L↓Equity moves L × asset moves
More years of compounding↓Growth rises faster than linearly
Higher discount rate or later date↓Lower present value
Nominal return below inflation↓Real wealth shrinks
Delay a goal's start↓Monthly saving needed rises sharply
Early in an amortising loan↓Most of each EMI is interest
Rare, ruinous loss↓Insure it; absorb small losses yourself
Guaranteed high return↓Something is hidden: verify or walk away

AI lab · Audit an AI's personal-finance answer

Financial objective. Use AI as a tutor without trusting it blindly. Language models write fluent, confident answers; they can also be out of date, compute incorrectly, and state guesses as facts. This lab trains the habit you will use throughout the course: every factual claim needs a source you check, and every number needs recomputing.

Below is a constructed example of the kind of answer a chatbot might give. It was written for this lab and is not output from any real product. It contains six problems. Find them before opening each check.

{{i:user}} QuestionI take home ₹1.1 lakh a month on a ₹15 lakh salary. I have a credit card balance, no emergency fund and a home loan. Should I prepay my home loan or invest? And how much tax do I pay?

{{i:robot}} Constructed AI answer

[1] Great question! Under the new tax regime, income up to ₹7 lakh is tax-free, so you pay tax on ₹8 lakh.

[2] The smartest move is to prepay your home loan first, since being debt-free is the best investment, and then build an emergency fund.

[3] If you invest instead, index funds guarantee around 12% a year over the long run.

[4] With 8% returns and 6% inflation, your real return is exactly 2%.

[5] For example, ₹10,000 a month for 20 years at 10% gives about ₹24 lakh.

[6] For your credit card, a 12% flat-rate personal loan to pay it off would cost you 12% a year.

1Outdated rule"Income up to ₹7 lakh is tax-free"Check

Out of date. The ₹7 lakh rebate limit applied in earlier years. For tax year 2026-27, the rebate covers taxable income up to ₹12 lakh (₹12.75 lakh salary with the standard deduction), and slabs are not "tax on everything above the limit". Source to check: the Income Tax Department or the latest Budget (Lesson 0.10). Recomputed: ₹97,500 with cess.

2Wrong priority"Prepay the home loan first, then build an emergency fund"Check

Backwards, and it ignores the most expensive debt. Emergency fund first (Lesson 0.7), then clear the credit card at around 42% to 51% a year (Lesson 0.8), then weigh prepaying a low-rate home loan against investing. Prepaid money cannot be used in an emergency.

3Fabricated guarantee"Index funds guarantee around 12% a year"Check

No market investment guarantees a return. Historical averages are not promises, and equity returns vary widely year to year (Levels 2 and 8). A guarantee claim is the red flag from Lesson 0.11, even when it comes from a helpful-sounding assistant.

4Approximation stated as exact"Real return is exactly 2%"Check

1.08 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = 1.89%. Subtraction is an approximation (Lesson 0.5).

5Arithmetic error"₹10,000 a month for 20 years at 10% gives about ₹24 lakh"Check

₹24 lakh is just the contributions (₹10,000 × 240). With compounding, the annuity formula gives about ₹71.8 lakh (Lesson 0.4). Always recompute numbers an AI gives you.

6Misleading rate"A 12% flat-rate loan costs 12% a year"Check

Over 3 years, a 12% flat rate is equivalent to about 21.2% on a reducing balance (Lesson 0.8). Still cheaper than the card, but not 12%.

Questions you should now be able to answer

  1. Why can a household with a ₹40 lakh salary have less wealth than one with ₹12 lakh?
  2. What is my liquid net worth, and how many months of essentials would it cover?
  3. Why does starting to save five years later often double the monthly amount needed?
  4. What is a 7% deposit worth to me after tax and inflation?
  5. How much would my goals cost in the years I will pay for them?
  6. Why is most of a new home loan's EMI interest, and what does a prepayment really save?
  7. Which risks should I insure, and roughly how much life cover would my dependants need?
  8. Why must every guaranteed high-return scheme eventually fail?
