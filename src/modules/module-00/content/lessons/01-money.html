Money, Income and Wealth

Big ideaIncome is a flow; wealth is a stock. You build wealth from the gap between what comes in and what goes out, kept over time, not from the size of the inflow.

Why this distinction exists

Everyone knows someone who earns well and always seems short of money, and someone on a modest salary who quietly owns a lot. Salary is the most visible number in a financial life, so we tend to use it as a proxy for how well someone is doing. It is a poor proxy. Two numbers describe a financial life, and they are measured in different ways.

[[income|Income]] is measured over a period: ₹1.1 lakh a month, ₹15 lakh a year. [[wealth|Wealth]] is measured on a date: what you own minus what you owe on 31 March. Confusing the two is the root of most personal-finance mistakes, from lifestyle creep to feeling rich because of a large EMI-financed flat.

The mental model: a bathtub

{{i:lightbulb}} Intuition

Picture a bathtub. The tap is your income. The drain is your spending. The water level is your wealth. Opening the tap wider does nothing for the level if the drain opens just as wide. The level rises only when inflow exceeds outflow, and it rises by the accumulated difference.

This is the [[stock-flow|stock and flow]] distinction, and it runs through all of finance. A company's balance sheet is a stock and its income statement is a flow (Lesson 3.5). A country's debt is a stock and its deficit is a flow (Lesson 6.14). Learn it once here, and you will see it everywhere.

The bathtub deliberately leaves out one thing: water that the tub grows by itself. In finance, that is the return on your wealth. We add it in Lesson 0.4, because it changes the picture dramatically.

Four pairs of ideas

Income vs wealth

Definition. Income is money received over a period. Wealth is the value of everything you own minus everything you owe, at a point in time.

Mechanism. Wealth at the end of a year equals wealth at the start, plus income, minus spending, plus any return on what you already hold. High income helps only through that equation. A ₹40 lakh salary with ₹40 lakh of spending builds no wealth at all.

Assets vs liabilities

An [[asset|asset]] is something you own that has economic value: cash, a fixed deposit, shares, a flat, a car, your provident fund. A [[liability|liability]] is something you owe: a home loan, a car loan, a credit-card balance. Wealth is assets minus liabilities. Lesson 0.3 turns this into a proper balance sheet; for now, notice that buying a flat with a loan adds a large asset and a large liability on the same day. Your wealth barely changes at the moment of purchase.

Consumption vs saving

Every rupee of income is either consumed or saved. [[consumption|Consumption]] is spending on things that are used up: rent, food, a holiday. [[saving|Saving]] is what is left. There is no third option: money you "didn't really spend" but can't account for was consumed.

Saving vs investing

Saving is a decision about how much not to spend. [[investing|Investing]] is a decision about what that saved money does. Cash in a savings account is saved but barely invested. Money in an equity fund is saved and invested, with more risk and a higher expected return. You can be an excellent saver and a poor investor, or the reverse; they are separate skills, and this curriculum treats them separately (investing starts properly in Level 2).

{{i:binoculars}} Investor lens

For most people in their twenties, the savings rate matters more than investment returns. A 2 percentage-point better return on ₹5 lakh is ₹10,000 a year. Saving an extra ₹10,000 a month is ₹1.2 lakh a year. Returns start to dominate only once the stock of wealth is large, which is exactly when the choices of Levels 2 to 9 begin to matter.

The same idea as an equation

{{i:function}} Formula · Wealth accumulation (no returns yet)

Wt=Wt−1+Yt−Ct
Wt
Wealth (net worth) at the end of period t, in ₹. A stock.
Yt
Income received during period t, in ₹ per period. A flow.
Ct
Consumption (spending) during period t, in ₹ per period. A flow.

Interpretation. Wealth changes each period by exactly the saving of that period, Y − C.

Worked example. Meera starts the year with ₹8.3 lakh of financial assets, takes home ₹13.2 lakh and spends ₹9.36 lakh (₹78,000 a month). Ignoring returns, she ends with 8.3 + 13.2 − 9.36 = ₹12.14 lakh.

Sensitivity. Every ₹1 of extra spending lowers end-of-year wealth by exactly ₹1. Over many years the effect grows, because Lesson 0.4 adds a return term r·Wt−1, and money not saved also never earns that return.

Limitations. No returns, no taxes, no price changes, no shocks. Each is added in a later lesson.

Two kinds of capital

[[financial-capital|Financial capital]] is what you have already accumulated: bank balances, investments, provident fund. [[human-capital|Human capital]] is your ability to earn in the future. It appears on no statement, yet for a young professional it is almost always the largest thing they own.

{{tag:hyp}}

Meera's financial capital is about ₹8.3 lakh (savings account, FD, mutual funds and EPF). Her human capital is the value today of roughly 31 more years of take-home pay. Valuing future income needs a tool from Lesson 0.4 (present value), so for now take the result on trust: under reasonable assumptions (take-home of ₹13.3 lakh a year, growing 2% to 3% a year faster than inflation, discounted at 4% to 5% a year after inflation) it comes to somewhere between ₹2.6 crore and ₹3.4 crore in today's money.

Financial capital₹8.3 lakh
Human capital≈ ₹3 crore

Interpretation. Over a career, human capital is gradually converted into financial capital: each month of work turns some future earning power into present savings. By retirement, human capital is close to zero and financial capital must do all the work. That single picture explains three later topics: why young people can usually take more investment risk (Lesson 8.28), why protecting income through insurance matters so much (Lesson 0.9), and why skills and health are investments too.

Rupees vs what rupees buy

A rupee is a claim on goods and services. If prices rise, each rupee claims less. The quantity a sum of money can buy is its [[purchasing-power|purchasing power]].

This gives two ways to measure any amount. [[nominal|Nominal]] values are in rupees of the day. [[real|Real]] values are adjusted for price changes, so they measure what the money can buy. If your savings grow from ₹10 lakh to ₹11 lakh in a year when prices rose 10%, your nominal wealth grew 10% and your real wealth did not grow at all.

That is all we need for now. Lesson 0.5 owns the details: how prices are measured, and the exact formula for real returns (which is not simple subtraction).

Common misconceptions

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“A high income means I am wealthy.”

Why this failsIncome is a flow and wealth is a stock. Without a persistent gap between income and spending, a high income produces no stock at all, and a high-spending household is often more fragile because its fixed costs are larger.

Better questionWhat is my net worth, and how much of each rupee of income do I keep?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“I save a lot, so I am investing.”

Why this failsSaving decides how much you keep. Investing decides what that money earns and what risks it carries. A large savings-account balance can lose purchasing power every year (Lesson 0.5).

Better questionWhat is my saved money doing, and does that match when I will need it?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“My flat is worth ₹1.2 crore, so I am fine.”

Why this failsWealth is assets minus liabilities, and a flat you live in cannot pay for groceries during a job loss. Lesson 0.3 separates net worth from liquid net worth for exactly this reason.

Better questionWhat is my net worth after the loan, and how much of it can I reach within a week?

Where this model breaks

  • No returns. The bathtub ignores growth on existing wealth. Over decades, returns can contribute more than contributions (Lesson 0.4).
  • No shocks. Real income stops, medical bills arrive, markets fall. Buffers and insurance exist for this (Lessons 0.7 and 0.9).
  • Human capital is uncertain. Careers end early, industries change, health fails. The ₹3 crore figure is an estimate built on assumptions, not an asset you can sell.
  • Valuing some assets is hard. Your flat, a family business or jewellery have no single price. Lesson 0.3 shows how to value them conservatively.

# A Classify each item as a stock or a flow: (a) rent paid in May, (b) the value of your mutual funds today, (c) EPF contributions this year, (d) your outstanding education loan. Savings rate = 18,000 ÷ 90,000 = 20% of take-home. # C A friend says: "My salary doubled in five years, so I'm twice as well off." What two numbers would you need to check whether that is true? A: Her spending (did it also double?) and the price level (inflation over five years). Being "twice as well off" is about real wealth and real saving, not nominal salary. # D Use the explorer. With ₹1,10,000 take-home, ₹78,000 spending and 8% income growth, what spending growth rate stops wealth from rising after about 15 years? A: About 10.5% a year (solve 78,000 × (1 + g)15 = 1,10,000 × 1.0815, which gives g ≈ 10.5%). At that rate spending catches up with income around year 15, so annual saving falls to zero and the wealth line flattens. The exact point depends on the starting gap: the larger the initial saving, the longer the gap takes to close. # E An advertisement says: "Your salary is your biggest asset, so borrow against it." Using the idea of human capital, explain what is right and what is dangerous in this claim. A: Right: human capital is usually a young person's largest asset. Dangerous: it is uncertain (job loss, illness), cannot be sold, and borrowing against it creates a fixed liability that must be paid even if the income stops. Debt converts an uncertain asset into a certain obligation. This is why Lesson 0.9 insists on protecting income before leveraging it.
