Why inflation changes every calculation

Lesson 0.4 grew money in rupees. But nobody wants rupees for their own sake; they want what rupees buy. If a plate of idli that costs ₹60 today costs ₹100 in ten years, then ₹100 in ten years is the same plate of idli. [[inflation|Inflation]] is the rate at which the general price level rises, and it quietly converts every future rupee into a smaller one.

{{i:lightbulb}} Intuition

Think of every rupee as a ticket that can be exchanged for a fixed share of a shopping basket. Inflation shrinks the share each ticket buys. Growing your number of tickets is only useful if it outpaces the shrinking. The real question for any investment is: does it grow my share of the basket?

What the Consumer Price Index measures

You cannot measure "the price level" directly. Statisticians instead price a fixed [[cpi|basket]] of goods and services that a typical household buys, every month, in many markets, and express the cost of the basket as an index. The period whose cost is set to 100 is the [[base-year|base year]].

Index, base year = 100. Next year = 100 × 16,970 ÷ 16,000 = 106.1. Inflation = 106.1 ÷ 100 − 1 = 6.1%.

Interpretation. Mobile prices rose 10% but barely moved the index; rent rose 6% and dominated it. Weights matter: an item's effect on inflation is its price change multiplied by its share of spending.

πt=CPItCPIt−1−1

Limitations. A fixed basket misses substitution (people switch to cheaper goods when prices rise), quality changes (a ₹15,000 phone today is not last decade's ₹15,000 phone) and new products. Statistical agencies correct for some of this and periodically update the basket.

Purchasing-power erosion

Inflation compounds just as returns do. If prices rise at π a year, the price level after n years is (1 + π)n times today's, and a fixed sum of money buys 1 ÷ (1 + π)n as much.

{{i:function}} Formula · Purchasing power and real value

Real value=Nominal value in year (1+π)n
Nominal value
Rupees in year n.
π
Average annual inflation over the period.
Real value
The same amount expressed in today's rupees (today's purchasing power).

Worked example. At 5% inflation, ₹1 crore in 25 years has the purchasing power of 1,00,00,000 ÷ 1.0525 = ₹29.5 lakh today. A retirement target that sounds large can be surprisingly modest in real terms.

Rule of thumb. At 6% inflation, prices double in about 12 years; at 4%, in about 18.

Nominal and real returns

A [[nominal|nominal]] return is the growth in rupees. A [[real-return|real return]] is the growth in purchasing power. The relationship is multiplicative, not additive, because inflation shrinks the value of the whole end balance, including the return itself.

{{i:function}} Formula · Real return (the Fisher relation)

1+rreal=1+rnominal1+πrreal≈rnominal−π
rnominal
Return in rupees over the period.
π
Inflation over the same period.
rreal
Return in purchasing power.

Why. ₹100 becomes ₹100(1 + rnominal) rupees, and each rupee then buys 1 ÷ (1 + π) of what it used to. Real growth factor = (1 + rnominal) ÷ (1 + π).

The approximation. Expanding shows rreal = (rnominal − π) ÷ (1 + π). Subtraction ignores the division by (1 + π), so it overstates the real return by roughly (rnominal − π) × π.

Worked examples. 7% and 5%: 1.905% (approximation 2%). 12% and 6%: 5.66% (approximation 6%). 20% and 15%: 4.35% (approximation 5%).

Interpretation. At Indian inflation rates of recent years the error is small, but it compounds over decades. In high-inflation settings (India in the early 1990s, or countries with double-digit inflation today) the approximation is misleading.

Inflation expectations

[[inflation-expectations|Inflation expectations]] are what people believe future inflation will be. They matter even before any price changes, because decisions made today embed them:

  • Interest rates. Lenders and depositors care about real returns, so the rate on a 5-year deposit reflects the inflation expected over those 5 years. Locking in a fixed rate is a bet that inflation will not rise much above what the rate already assumes.
  • Wages and prices. If workers expect 8% inflation they bargain for raises that cover it, and firms set prices expecting higher costs. Expectations can partly fulfil themselves.
  • Your plans. Every goal in Lesson 0.6 needs an inflation assumption. It is an expectation, and it should be revisited, not treated as a fact.

This is why central banks work hard to anchor expectations. India's [[inflation-target|inflation target]] of 4% (tolerance band 2% to 6%) gives households and firms a reference point. How the RBI uses its policy rate to steer inflation, and how markets react to data relative to expectations, is the subject of Lessons 6.6 to 6.8 and 6.23.

{{i:binoculars}} Investor lens · Your personal inflation

CPI is an average basket. Your own inflation depends on what you buy. Households spending heavily on private education, health care or urban rent often experience faster price rises than the headline number; a household whose biggest costs are food and fuel feels those swings more sharply. For long-term goals, planners often use a higher inflation assumption for education and medical costs than for general spending. That is an assumption to justify, not a rule.

Common misconceptions

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“Inflation has fallen, so prices will come down.”

Why this failsInflation is the rate of change of prices. Falling inflation from 6% to 4% means prices are still rising, just more slowly. Prices falling across the board (deflation) is rare and usually a sign of economic trouble.

Better questionHow much higher will prices be in a year, and is my income keeping up?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“My savings account is safe, so my money is safe.”

Why this failsA deposit is protected against default (up to deposit-insurance limits, Lesson 0.7) but not against inflation. A 3% account during 5% inflation loses about 1.9% of purchasing power a year, roughly 17% over ten years.

Better questionWhat is the real return, and is this money meant for the short term (where stability matters most) or the long term (where purchasing power matters most)?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“CPI inflation was 4.5%, so my costs rose 4.5%.”

Why this failsCPI prices an average basket. Your rent, school fees or medicines may rise at very different rates.

Better questionWhat did my own largest expenses cost a year ago?

Where this breaks

  • Averages hide spread. Food inflation can run far above or below headline inflation in a given month, and rural and urban rates differ.
  • Series changes. The 2024 base revision changed weights and items. Linking old and new series needs the official linking factor.
  • Quality and new goods. Price indices struggle with products that improve or did not exist in the base year.
  • Expectations are not forecasts. A 5% assumption over 20 years is a planning choice. Actual inflation will differ, sometimes by a lot, so plans need margins and reviews.

Practice ladder

# A True or false: (a) CPI measures the prices of shares and property. (b) A base year has an index value of 100. (c) If CPI goes from 120 to 126, inflation was 6%. A: (a) False: CPI prices consumer goods and services (housing enters through rent, not property prices). (b) True. (c) False: 126 ÷ 120 − 1 = 5%. The index rose 6 points, not 6%. # B Using the Economic Survey rates for 2014-15 to 2019-20, what would a salary of ₹6 lakh in 2014 need to be in 2020 to keep the same purchasing power? A: 6,00,000 × 1.059 × 1.049 × 1.045 × 1.036 × 1.034 × 1.048 = 6,00,000 × 1.3033 ≈ ₹7.82 lakh. A 30% nominal raise over six years was only enough to stand still. # C A 5-year deposit pays 7.25% when inflation expectations are 4.5%. Two years later, inflation settles at 6.5%. Who gained and who lost relative to expectations: the depositor or the bank? A: The depositor expected a real return of about 2.6% (1.0725 ÷ 1.045 − 1) but receives about 0.7% (1.0725 ÷ 1.065 − 1). The bank gained: it is repaying in rupees that buy less than expected. Unexpected inflation transfers purchasing power from lenders to borrowers. # D Meera's rent is ₹28,000 and rises 8% a year; her other spending rises with CPI at 5%. Estimate her personal inflation rate for next year. A: Rent is 28,000 of her 78,000 spending, about 36%. Personal inflation ≈ 0.36 × 8% + 0.64 × 5% ≈ 6.1%, a full point above CPI. Rent, the heaviest line, dominates. # E A banner says: "Our fund returned 11% while inflation was only 6%, so investors earned a 5% real return." Identify two problems with the statement. A: (1) The real return is 1.11 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = 4.7%, not 5% (small but systematic). (2) More importantly, the figure is before tax and before any exit costs, and it describes one past period; it says nothing about risk or whether it will persist. A more careful claim: "in this period, the fund's return exceeded inflation by about 4.7% a year before taxes."
