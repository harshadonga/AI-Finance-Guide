Financial Goals

Big ideaA goal is a future cost in future rupees. Its horizon decides how much short-term volatility you can live with, and the start date is the most powerful lever you control.

Why name your goals

"Save as much as possible" is not a plan. It gives no way to know whether you are saving enough, too much, or into the wrong things. A goal with an amount and a date turns saving into arithmetic: you can compute what is needed, check progress, and see the cost of changing your mind.

Goals also answer a question Lesson 0.1 left open: saving decides how much, but when the money is needed decides what it should be doing. Money needed in eighteen months and money needed in thirty years should not be invested the same way.

{{i:lightbulb}} Intuition

Treat each goal as a dated bill you owe your future self. Like any bill, it has an amount and a due date. Unlike most bills, its amount keeps growing with inflation until it is paid, and you can pay it in instalments that earn a return while they wait.

Short, medium and long-term goals

The [[goal-horizon|horizon]] is the time between today and the date the money is needed. The boundaries below are a teaching convention, not a rule; what matters is the logic behind them.

{{i:timer}} Short term under about 3 years

A trip, a car, a wedding contribution. There is no time to recover from a fall in value, so stability of capital matters most. Expected return is secondary.

{{i:calendar}} Medium term about 3 to 7 years

A down payment, a career break, a master's degree. Some volatility can be tolerated, but a bad year near the date could force a delay. A blend of stable and growth assets is common.

{{i:mountains}} Long term more than about 7 years

A child's education, retirement. There is time for bad years to be followed by recoveries, and inflation does the most damage, so purchasing power growth matters most.

Which assets suit each horizon is the subject of Levels 2 and 8 (asset classes, then allocation). Here we build the arithmetic that any of those choices must satisfy.

Roughly double: ₹15,923 a month now, or ₹31,743 a month from year 5. They would also contribute more in total: ₹38.1 lakh instead of ₹28.7 lakh. The worked example below shows where these numbers come from.

The method: three steps

  1. Price the goal today. What would it cost if you paid now? Use a realistic quote, not a guess.
  2. Inflate it to the due date. Future cost = today's cost × (1 + goal inflation)years. Choose the inflation rate for this kind of expense, which may differ from CPI (Lesson 0.5).
  3. Work out the saving needed. Subtract what existing savings for this goal will grow to, then solve for the monthly contribution with the annuity formula from Lesson 0.4.

{{i:function}} Formula · Required monthly contribution

C=(G0(1+g)n−S(1+r)n)×i(1+i)12n−1
G0
Cost of the goal in today's rupees.
g
Annual inflation rate for this goal.
n
Years until the goal.
S
Savings already set aside for this goal, in ₹.
r, i
Assumed annual return, and the equivalent monthly rate i = (1 + r)1/12 − 1.
C
Level monthly contribution needed, in ₹, paid at the end of each month.

Interpretation. The numerator is the gap: future cost minus what existing savings will become. The rest spreads that gap over 12n monthly contributions that each earn a return until the due date.

Sensitivity. The result is most sensitive to n (through both the inflated cost and the number of compounding months), then to r − g, the real return. A higher assumed return lowers C only if it is realistic for the horizon.

Limitations. Assumes a constant return and constant inflation. Real outcomes vary; the plan must be reviewed yearly and adjusted.

Worked example: education

{{i:calculator}} Worked example · The Nairs' education goal

{{tag:hyp}}

The Nairs' daughter is 3. A four-year degree they have in mind would cost about ₹20 lakh today. Education costs have tended to rise faster than general prices, so they assume 8% a year education inflation. The money is needed in 15 years. They assume a 10% a year return on long-term savings. They have nothing set aside yet.

  1. Future cost = 20,00,000 × 1.0815 = ₹63.44 lakh.
  2. Monthly rate i = 1.101/12 − 1 = 0.797%. Months = 180.
  3. C = 63,44,338 × 0.00797 ÷ (1.00797180 − 1) = ₹15,923 a month. Total contributed: ₹28.7 lakh. Growth supplies the other ₹34.8 lakh.
  4. If they start in 5 years (120 months): C = ₹31,743 a month, and ₹38.1 lakh contributed.

Interpretation. Delay doubles the monthly burden. The 8% inflation assumption also matters enormously: at 6%, the future cost would be ₹47.9 lakh and the monthly amount about ₹12,000.

{{i:chart-line}} Quant lens · Planning in real terms

An equivalent method works in today's rupees throughout. The real return is 1.10 ÷ 1.08 − 1 = 1.85% a year. Saving towards ₹20 lakh (in today's money) at 1.85% a year needs about ₹9,660 a month in today's rupees, with the amount raised each year by education inflation. It starts lower than ₹15,923 and ends much higher, which is how a "step-up" SIP works. Both paths reach the same goal; they spread the effort differently across time.

Retirement: the largest and least certain goal

Retirement is different from other goals in two ways. It is not one payment but decades of spending with no salary, so the target is itself a present value (Lesson 0.4). And it is so far away that small changes in assumptions move the answer by crores. The right attitude is an order-of-magnitude estimate, revisited every year.

{{i:calculator}} Worked example · Meera's rough retirement number

{{tag:hyp}}

Assumptions, all debatable: she wants ₹60,000 a month of spending in today's money from 60, for 25 years. Inflation 5% a year. In retirement, her savings earn 2% a year above inflation. Before retirement, 10% a year nominal.

  1. Annual spending in today's money: ₹7.2 lakh.
  2. Corpus needed at 60, in today's money: the present value of ₹7.2 lakh a year for 25 years at a 2% real rate = ₹1.41 crore.
  3. In rupees of the year she turns 60 (31 years away): 1.41 crore × 1.0531 ≈ ₹6.4 crore. Her monthly spending then would be about ₹2.7 lakh in that year's rupees.
  4. Her existing EPF and funds (₹5.5 lakh) might grow to about ₹1.06 crore at 10%. The rest needs about ₹23,000 a month for 31 years, before counting her continuing EPF contributions, which would reduce it.

Interpretation. The ₹6.4 crore figure sounds impossible; the ₹23,000 a month looks achievable for someone saving ₹32,000 today. Both describe the same plan. What would break it: lower returns, higher inflation, retiring earlier, or living longer. Lessons 8.28 (lifecycle investing) and 9.7 (scenario analysis) return to these risks.

{{i:warning}} Caution

Retirement calculators produce precise-looking numbers from assumptions that are anything but precise. Change the real return in retirement from 2% to 0% and the corpus rises by about 28%. Treat any single figure as the centre of a wide range.

Housing and major purchases

A down payment

Meera wants to buy a ₹90 lakh flat (today's price) in 5 years with a 20% down payment. If flat prices rise 6% a year, the flat will cost ₹1.20 crore and the down payment ₹24.1 lakh. With a medium horizon she assumes a more conservative 7% return: that needs about ₹33,800 a month, more than she saves today. The arithmetic forces a real decision: raise saving, extend the date, choose a cheaper flat, or reconsider buying now.

Whether to rent or buy is a genuine question with no universal answer. It depends on the price-to-rent ratio, how long you will stay, the interest rate, and how much flexibility is worth to you. The tools here let you compute the cost of each path; they do not decide which is better.

A car in two years

A car costing ₹8 lakh today, rising 5% a year, will cost ₹8.82 lakh in two years. At 6.5% in a deposit, that needs about ₹34,600 a month for 24 months. With a two-year horizon, the return barely matters; the saving does. That is the signature of a short-term goal.

Goal-based investing

[[goal-based-investing|Goal-based investing]] organises saving around specific dated goals, each with its own amount, horizon and tolerance for volatility, instead of one undifferentiated pot. Its strengths are practical: it makes progress measurable, and it stops short-term money from being exposed to long-term risks.

A commonly used order of priorities, before goal saving begins, is: an emergency fund (Lesson 0.7), adequate insurance (Lesson 0.9), and paying off expensive consumer debt (Lesson 0.8). A goal plan built on top of a 42% credit-card balance is not a plan.

{{i:binoculars}} Investor lens

Separate "buckets" are a mental convenience; money is interchangeable. Treating goals separately can lead to odd results, such as holding a low-yielding deposit for one goal while paying high interest on a loan for another. Lesson 9 calls this mental accounting. Use goals to organise the plan, and check the whole balance sheet for contradictions.

Common misconceptions

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“Education will cost ₹20 lakh, so I need to save ₹20 lakh.”

Why this fails₹20 lakh is today's price. At 8% education inflation over 15 years, it becomes ₹63 lakh. Planning in today's prices while investing in future rupees leaves a gap of two-thirds.

Better questionWhat will this cost in the year I pay for it?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“If I assume a 15% return, the monthly amount becomes affordable.”

Why this failsThe assumed return must be realistic for the horizon and the assets that can actually deliver it, with their volatility. Raising the assumption lowers the number on the screen, not the risk in the world.

Better questionWhat return is reasonable for this horizon, and what do I do if actual returns fall short?

Where this breaks

  • Returns are not constant. A bad market just before the goal date can leave a gap with no time to recover (sequence risk). This is why money for near goals should move to stable assets as the date approaches (Lesson 8.28).
  • Goals change. Children choose different courses, families move cities. Plans must be revised, not merely followed.
  • Tax is ignored here. After-tax returns are lower; Lesson 0.10 shows by how much.
  • Competing goals. The monthly amounts for all goals must fit inside free cash flow (Lesson 0.2). When they do not, priorities have to be set explicitly.
