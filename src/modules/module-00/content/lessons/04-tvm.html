Time Value of Money

Big ideaA rupee today and a rupee in ten years are different goods. Compounding makes growth depend more on time than on rate, and discounting is simply compounding run backwards.

Why money has a time value

Would you rather have ₹1 lakh today or ₹1 lakh in five years? Almost everyone chooses today, and for good reasons. Money today can be invested and grow. Prices may rise, so the same rupees may buy less later (Lesson 0.5). And the future is uncertain: a promise of money later might not be kept.

Because of the first reason alone, ₹1 lakh today is worth more than ₹1 lakh later. The question this lesson answers is: how much more? The same tools will later value bonds (Lesson 7), companies (Lesson 5) and your own future income (Lesson 0.9).

{{i:lightbulb}} Intuition

Two pictures carry the whole lesson. A snowball: as it rolls it picks up snow, and a bigger ball picks up more snow per turn, so growth itself grows. A time machine: future value carries money forward in time by growing it; present value carries money back by shrinking it at the same rate. Every formula below is one of these two pictures.

Now a second, sharper question: at 12%, ₹1 lakh becomes ₹3.1 lakh in 10 years and ₹9.6 lakh in 20. Doubling the time more than quadrupled the gain (₹2.1 lakh to ₹8.6 lakh). That is what exponential growth means in practice.

Simple interest

[[simple-interest|Simple interest]] is paid only on the original amount. Interest is taken out, not added to the balance, so the base never grows.

{{i:function}} Formula · Simple interest

I=P×r×nFV=P(1+rn)
P
Principal: the amount invested or borrowed, in ₹.
r
Interest rate per period, as a decimal (12% = 0.12).
n
Number of periods (years, if r is annual).
I
Total interest, in ₹.

Worked example. ₹1,00,000 at 12% for 30 years: I = 1,00,000 × 0.12 × 30 = ₹3,60,000; total ₹4,60,000.

Where you meet it. Deposits that pay interest out monthly or quarterly instead of reinvesting it, some bonds' coupon payments, and the misleading "flat rate" loans of Lesson 0.8.

Compound interest and future value

With [[compound-interest|compound interest]], each period's interest is added to the balance and earns interest itself. After one year ₹1,00,000 at 12% becomes ₹1,12,000. In year two, 12% is earned on ₹1,12,000, giving ₹1,25,440. The extra ₹1,440 is interest on interest, and it grows every year.

{{i:function}} Formula · Future value

FV=PV×(1+r)n
FV
[[future-value|Future value]] after n periods, in ₹.
PV
Present value: the amount today, in ₹.
r
Return per period, as a decimal.
n
Number of periods. The exponent is what makes growth accelerate.

Worked example. ₹1,00,000 at 12%: 10 years → ₹3,10,585; 20 years → ₹9,64,629; 30 years → ₹29,95,992.

Sensitivity to rate. At 30 years, moving from 10% to 12% raises the result from ₹17.4 lakh to ₹30.0 lakh: a 2-point change in rate, a 72% change in outcome. Small differences in return, or in fees, matter enormously over long horizons (Lesson 2.6 on expense ratios).

Sensitivity to time. Each extra year multiplies by (1 + r), so the rupee gain from one more year is largest at the end. The final 10 of 30 years produced ₹20.3 lakh of the ₹29 lakh total gain.

Limitations. Assumes a constant return and full reinvestment, no withdrawals, taxes or fees. Real investment returns vary year to year; Lesson 8.3 shows why the order of returns then matters.

Compounding more often

Rates are usually quoted per year, but interest may be added more often. Many Indian bank fixed deposits add interest quarterly. A quoted 7% a year compounded quarterly means 1.75% per quarter, which grows to (1.0175)4 − 1 = 7.19% over a year. That true yearly figure is the effective annual rate, covered below under annualisation.

Present value and discounting

Run the time machine backwards. If ₹1 today grows to ₹(1 + r)n, then a rupee received n years from now is worth 1 ÷ (1 + r)n today. Converting a future amount into today's value is called [[discounting|discounting]], and the rate used is the [[discount-rate|discount rate]].

{{i:function}} Formula · Present value

PV=FV(1+r)n
PV
[[present-value|Present value]]: what the future amount is worth today, in ₹.
FV
The future amount, in ₹.
r
Discount rate per period: the return you could earn elsewhere on money of similar risk.
n
Periods until the money arrives.

Worked example. You need ₹10 lakh in 8 years and can earn 7% a year safely. PV = 10,00,000 ÷ 1.078 = ₹5,82,009. That is the amount to set aside today.

Interpretation. Present value lets you compare money at different dates on one scale. It is the foundation of every valuation in this course: a bond is the present value of its payments, a company the present value of its future cash flows.

Sensitivity. The further away the money and the higher the rate, the smaller its present value. At 12% instead of 7%, the same ₹10 lakh in 8 years is worth only ₹4,03,883 today.

Limitations. The answer depends heavily on the discount rate, which is a judgement. Choosing it is one of the hardest problems in finance (Lessons 5.9 to 5.11).

Regular contributions: the annuity formulas

Most real saving is a flow: a fixed amount every month. A series of equal payments at regular intervals is called an [[annuity|annuity]]. A monthly SIP is one; so is an EMI. Adding up the future value of each payment gives a compact formula, needed again in Lessons 0.6 and 0.8.

{{i:function}} Formula · Future value of a level contribution (paid at the end of each period)

FV=C×(1+i)N−1i
C
Contribution per period, in ₹ (for example per month).
i
Return per period (monthly rate if C is monthly).
N
Number of contributions.

Where it comes from. The last payment earns nothing, the one before earns one period, and the first earns N − 1 periods: C[1 + (1+i) + … + (1+i)N−1]. That geometric series sums to the fraction shown.

Converting an annual rate to monthly. Use i = (1 + r)1/12 − 1, not r ÷ 12, when r is an effective annual return. At 10% a year, i = 0.797% a month (not 0.833%).

Worked example. ₹10,000 a month for 25 years at 10% a year: i = 0.797%, N = 300, FV ≈ ₹1.23 crore, from ₹30 lakh contributed.

Rearranged. To reach a target FV, the contribution needed is C = FV × i ÷ ((1 + i)N − 1). Lesson 0.6 uses exactly this.

CAGR: one rate for a bumpy path

Investments rarely grow at a steady rate. [[cagr|CAGR]] (compound annual growth rate) answers: what constant yearly rate would have taken the starting value to the ending value over the same period? It is the smooth path that ends in the same place as the bumpy real one.

{{i:function}} Formula · CAGR

CAGR=(End valueStart value)1/n−1
n
Number of years between the two values (can be fractional: 30 months = 2.5).
1/n power
The n-th root: "which yearly growth factor, multiplied n times, gives the total growth?"

Worked example. A portfolio grows from ₹2,00,000 to ₹3,40,000 in 5 years. Total growth = 70%. CAGR = 1.71/5 − 1 = 11.2% a year. Dividing 70% by 5 gives 14%, which overstates the yearly rate because it ignores compounding.

Limitations. CAGR uses only two points. It says nothing about the path: a smooth 11% and a ride through a 40% crash can have the same CAGR. It also ignores money added or withdrawn along the way; for that you need XIRR (Lesson 8.5).

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“My fund returned +50% and then −50%, so on average it broke even.”

Why this fails₹1,00,000 × 1.5 = ₹1,50,000; × 0.5 = ₹75,000. You lost 25%. The arithmetic average of the two returns is 0%; the compound (geometric) result is −13.4% a year. Losses need larger gains to recover: a 50% fall needs a 100% rise to get back.

Better questionWhat was the CAGR from start to end?

Annualisation

[[annualisation|Annualisation]] converts a rate for one period into its yearly equivalent so that different offers can be compared. There are two ways, and the difference is exactly compounding.

{{i:function}} Formula · Effective annual rate

EAR=(1+rperiod)m−1
rperiod
Rate for one period (a month, a quarter).
m
Number of such periods in a year (12, 4).
EAR
[[ear|Effective annual rate]]: the true yearly growth, including compounding within the year.

Worked examples. 1% a month: simple annualisation says 12%; EAR = 1.0112 − 1 = 12.68%. A credit card at 3.5% a month is quoted as 42% a year, but an unpaid balance grows at 1.03512 − 1 = 51.1% a year.

Interpretation. Always compare offers on the same basis. A lender quoting a monthly rate and a deposit quoting an annual rate compounded quarterly can only be compared after converting both to EARs.

{{i:warning}} Caution · Annualising short periods

A fund that rose 10% in three months has an annualised return of 1.14 − 1 = 46.4%. That number is arithmetically correct and practically meaningless: it assumes the next nine months will repeat the best quarter. Treat any annualised figure from less than a year of data as a curiosity, not evidence. Lesson 15 returns to this with backtests.

Opportunity cost

The [[opportunity-cost|opportunity cost]] of a choice is the value of the best alternative you give up. With money, the alternative is usually "investing it instead", which is why the discount rate in present-value problems is often called the opportunity cost of capital.

{{i:calculator}} Worked example · The ₹2 lakh upgrade

{{tag:hyp}}

At 29, Meera considers spending ₹2,00,000 on a car upgrade. If she invested it instead at an assumed 10% a year for 20 years, it would become 2,00,000 × 1.120 = ₹13.45 lakh at 49. With 5% inflation, that is worth about 2,00,000 × (1.10 ÷ 1.05)20 ≈ ₹5.07 lakh in today's money.

Interpretation. This does not mean she should never buy anything. It means the real price of the upgrade is not ₹2 lakh but about ₹5 lakh of future purchasing power, and that is the number to weigh against the pleasure and usefulness of the car. Opportunity cost makes the trade-off visible; it does not make the choice.

You will meet opportunity cost again in each lesson that follows: the cost of holding an emergency fund in low-yielding deposits (Lesson 0.7), the choice between prepaying a loan and investing (Lesson 0.8), and a company's choice of how to use its cash (Lesson 4.13).

{{i:book-open}} Advanced note · The rule of 72 {{i:caret-right}}

Money doubles in roughly 72 ÷ (rate in %) years: about 7.2 years at 10%, 6 years at 12%, 10 years at 7.2%. It works because (1 + r)n = 2 when n = ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + r) ≈ 0.693 ÷ r, and 72 is a convenient number near 69.3 × 1.04 that corrects for the approximation at typical rates.

It is accurate within a few months between about 6% and 12%. At 1% it says 72 years (true answer 69.7); at 36% it says 2 years (true answer 2.25). Use it for mental estimates, never for decisions. Logarithms are covered formally in Lesson 12.3.

Common misconceptions

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“Starting 5 years later only costs me 5 years of contributions.”

Why this failsIt costs the last 5 years of compounding, which are the largest. In the explorer's default case, cutting 25 years to 20 lowers the final value from about ₹1.34 crore to ₹79 lakh, while contributions fall only from ₹31 lakh to ₹25 lakh.

Better questionWhat is the smallest amount I can start with now?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“2% a month and 24% a year are the same.”

Why this fails2% a month compounds to 26.8% a year. The gap is larger for higher rates and more frequent compounding. Lenders prefer quoting the smaller-looking number; savers should convert everything to effective annual rates.

Better questionWhat is the effective annual rate?

Where this breaks

  • Constant returns are fiction. Risky investments do not earn a fixed rate. The formulas describe an average path; actual outcomes spread widely around it, and the spread grows with time (Lesson 14.20 simulates this).
  • Nominal vs real. A ₹1.23 crore future value is in future rupees. Lesson 0.5 shows how to convert it into today's purchasing power.
  • Taxes and costs. Tax on returns and annual fees reduce the effective compounding rate every year (Lesson 0.10).
  • The discount rate is a choice. Present values can be pushed around by changing it. Always ask what rate was used and why.

Practice ladder

# A Which of these grows faster over 20 years: ₹1 lakh at 8% simple interest, or ₹1 lakh at 6% compound interest? A: Simple 8%: ₹1 lakh × (1 + 0.08 × 20) = ₹2.6 lakh. Compound 6%: ₹1 lakh × 1.0620 = ₹3.21 lakh. Compounding at the lower rate wins over 20 years; over 5 years the simple 8% would win (₹1.40 lakh vs ₹1.34 lakh). # B A bank offers a 5-year FD at 7.1% a year compounded quarterly. What is ₹3,00,000 worth at maturity, and what is the effective annual rate? A: Quarterly rate = 1.775%, 20 quarters. FV = 3,00,000 × 1.0177520 = ₹4,26,524. EAR = 1.017754 − 1 = 7.29%. (Tax on the interest is ignored here; see Lesson 0.10.) # C A mutual-fund advertisement shows "3-year return: 62%". Convert this to something comparable with a 7% FD and explain what the number does not tell you. A: CAGR = 1.621/3 − 1 = 17.4% a year. It does not tell you how bumpy the path was, whether that period was unusually good, how the fund compares with its benchmark, or what fees and taxes did. Three years of past returns say little about the next three (Lesson 2.6 and Lesson 9.27 on survivorship). # D You will receive ₹5 lakh bonuses at the end of each of the next 3 years. At a 9% discount rate, what are they worth today in total? A: 5 ÷ 1.09 + 5 ÷ 1.092 + 5 ÷ 1.093 = 4.587 + 4.208 + 3.861 = ₹12.66 lakh, not ₹15 lakh. (Equivalently, the present value of a 3-payment annuity.) # E A scheme says: "Invest ₹1 lakh, get ₹2 lakh in 3 years, guaranteed." Compute the implied CAGR and explain why that number alone should make you suspicious. A: CAGR = 21/3 − 1 = 26% a year, guaranteed. Government securities and bank deposits, the safest rupee assets, pay far less. A guaranteed rate several times the safe rate implies either huge hidden risk or no real investment at all. Lesson 0.11 shows how such schemes pay early investors and then collapse.
