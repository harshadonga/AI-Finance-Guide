Lesson 0.7 · Part III · Plans and buffers

Emergency Funds

Big ideaAn emergency fund is insurance you pay for with lower returns. It buys time, so that a shock does not force you to sell investments at bad prices or borrow at 40% a year.

Why liquidity deserves its own lesson

Shocks arrive without warning: a layoff, a hospital admission not fully covered, an urgent trip home, a car accident. When one arrives, the question is not "how rich am I?" but "what can I pay with this week?" Lesson 0.3 called that [[liquidity|liquidity]], and showed how a household can have a large net worth and almost none of it within reach.

Without a buffer, a shock forces one of three bad options: borrow on a credit card or a quick loan at very high rates, sell investments at whatever price the market offers that week, or break long-term commitments such as an insurance policy or a retirement account. An [[emergency-fund|emergency fund]] exists to avoid all three.

{{i:lightbulb}} Intuition

Think of it as runway: the number of months you could keep paying essential bills if income stopped today. A plane with a short runway must get everything right on take-off. A household with a long runway can take a bad month, look for the right next job instead of the first one, and leave its investments alone.

Sizing: a method, not a single rule

The standard advice of "three to six months of expenses" is a reasonable starting point with two problems. It does not say which expenses, and it ignores the circumstances that make a household more or less exposed.

Step 1: measure essential outflow

Use essential costs, not total spending, because in a crisis the discretionary lines stop. From Lesson 0.2, Meera's fixed and necessary variable costs are ₹55,000 a month. Add any costs a crisis would create, such as the health-insurance premium if an employer's group cover ends with the job.

Worked example. Meera: salaried in tech, no dependants, a personal health policy, single earner. Six months × ₹55,000 = ₹3.3 lakh. Her stable liquid assets today are ₹1.95 lakh after clearing the card (Lesson 0.3), a runway of about 3.5 months: she is short by about ₹1.35 lakh.

Interpretation. Runway counts only money that is both reachable and stable in value. Equity funds are reachable but can be down 30% in exactly the month you need them.

The cost of safety

Safety is not free. Money kept stable and instantly available usually earns less than money invested for the long term. That difference is the [[opportunity-cost|opportunity cost]] of the fund (Lesson 0.4), and it is worth measuring rather than ignoring or exaggerating.

{{i:calculator}} Worked example · What Meera's buffer costs

{{tag:hyp}}

Suppose her ₹3.3 lakh fund earns 3.5 percentage points a year less than a long-term investment would (an assumption). The cost is about 3,30,000 × 0.035 = ₹11,550 a year before tax, roughly ₹960 a month.

Compare that with the alternative. A three-month income gap without a fund means about ₹1.65 lakh on a credit card. At 3.5% a month, a single month of that balance costs about ₹5,800 in interest, and balances like that rarely clear in a month. Or she sells equity funds after a 25% market fall and locks in the loss.

Interpretation. The fund's cost is small, predictable and paid in good times. The cost of not having one is large, uncertain and paid at the worst moment. That asymmetry is the same logic as insurance (Lesson 0.9).

{{i:warning}} Caution

Too large a buffer has a real cost too. A household holding three years of expenses in a savings account while inflation runs at 5% is paying a high price for safety it could get more cheaply through insurance and a moderately sized fund. Size the fund deliberately, then invest the rest for its goals.

Where to keep it

The fund has three requirements in order: its value must not fall, you must be able to reach it within a day or two, and only then should it earn as much as possible. That order rules out most growth assets. The instruments below are named here only for their liquidity and stability; their mechanics are taught in Level 2.

{{i:seal-check}} Current fact · Deposit insurance

{{tag:curr:29 Sep 2026}}

The [[dicgc|Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation]] insures bank deposits (savings, fixed, recurring and current) up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank, including principal and interest. All accounts you hold at one bank "in the same right and the same capacity" are added together for this limit, across all its branches. An emergency fund larger than ₹5 lakh can be spread across more than one bank to stay within the cover.

{{i:seal-check}} Verified 29 Sep 2026 · Source: DICGC information booklet

A practical layered structure: about one month of essentials in the savings account for instant needs, and the rest in a sweep-in deposit or a laddered set of FDs (or, once you understand them, a liquid fund).

{{i:clock-counter-clockwise}} Historical case · March 2020

When income and markets fall together

{{tag:hist:2020}}

{{i:eye}} What households knew at the time

On 24 March 2020, India announced a nationwide lockdown in response to COVID-19. Stock markets in India and around the world had already fallen sharply that month. For many households, the income shock and the market shock arrived in the same weeks.

A household relying on its equity funds as a buffer faced a choice: sell after a steep fall, or borrow. Nobody knew then how long the lockdown would last, whether jobs would return, or when markets would recover.

{{i:clock-clockwise}} What we learned afterwards

Markets recovered faster than many expected, and investors who were able to hold on benefited. Households that had to sell near the lows did not. The ability to wait depended heavily on having a stable buffer and secure income.

The episode is a clear example of correlated risk: the bad events that trigger an emergency and the bad prices that make selling costly tend to happen together.

{{i:lightbulb}} Lessons

  • An emergency fund should not depend on markets being calm when you need it.
  • Runway buys the right to wait, which is often worth more than the return forgone.
  • Knowing the recovery afterwards makes the choice look easy; at the time, it was not.

Common misconceptions

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“My credit-card limit is my emergency fund.”

Why this failsA credit limit is borrowed money at around 40% to 50% a year once balances revolve, and it can be reduced by the issuer exactly when your finances look weaker. It turns a temporary shock into long-lasting debt.

Better questionHow many months could I cover without borrowing anything?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“My equity funds can be sold in two days, so they are my emergency fund.”

Why this failsLiquid is not the same as stable. Emergencies and market falls often coincide (see March 2020). You would be selling at the worst time.

Better questionWhat would this be worth in a bad month, and would I be willing to sell then?

Where this breaks

  • Rules of thumb ignore circumstances. The explorer's month counts are a teaching heuristic. A household with a chronic illness, a family business or an ageing parent may need much more.
  • Not every shock is an income loss. A large medical bill is a one-off outflow; insurance is the main defence, and the fund covers deductibles and gaps.
  • The fund gets raided. Sales, holidays and gadgets are not emergencies. Keeping the fund in a separate account with a boring name helps.
  • Inflation erodes it. Essential costs rise every year; revisit the target annually.

Using nine months, what is her target? She has ₹1.8 lakh; how long would it take to reach the target saving ₹25,000 a month? A: Target = 9 × 45,000 = ₹4.05 lakh. Gap = ₹2.25 lakh. At ₹25,000 a month: 9 months (ignoring interest). # C Meera's runway is 3.5 months. A friend says, "You'll get another job in a month, don't bother." How would you evaluate that? A: One month might be the typical case, but the fund exists for the bad case: a slow hiring market, a health issue, a correlated downturn. The cost of holding 6 months is about ₹11,550 a year; the cost of running out is high-interest debt or a forced sale. Planning for the typical case is how buffers fail. # D A couple has one stable government salary and one start-up salary, essentials of ₹1.1 lakh, and a child. Suggest a target and a structure. A: Two incomes lower the risk of a total loss; the start-up role raises the risk of one. A reasonable target is around 6 months of essentials (₹6.6 lakh), with one month in a savings account and the rest in sweep-in deposits split across two banks to stay within the ₹5 lakh insurance limit per bank. Reasoning matters more than the exact number. # E An app offers "an emergency fund that earns 12%!" by investing your buffer in a mix of equity and corporate bonds. Critique it. A: It breaks the first requirement: stability of value. A 12% expected return comes with volatility and possible credit losses, and those tend to strike in exactly the conditions that cause emergencies. The pitch optimises the least important requirement (return) at the expense of the most important (stability).
