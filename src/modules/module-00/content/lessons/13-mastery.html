Understanding

? Last year, Reza's income was ₹18 lakh and his net worth rose by ₹1.5 lakh. Which statement must be true (ignoring investment returns)? - He saved ₹18 lakh || Income is the inflow; saving is income minus spending. + His spending was about ₹16.5 lakh || Net worth changes by income minus spending: 18 − 16.5 = 1.5. - His wealth is ₹18 lakh || Income (a flow) and wealth (a stock) are different quantities. ? Your savings grew 6% last year while prices rose 6%. What happened to your wealth? - It grew 6% || That is the nominal change, in rupees. + Its purchasing power was unchanged || Real growth = 1.06 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = 0%. - It fell 6% || It would fall only if the rupee balance had stayed flat. ? A friend's employer offers ₹1 lakh extra salary. Her average tax rate is 9% and her marginal rate is 20.8%. How much extra tax will she pay, under the simplified model? - About ₹9,000 || The average rate applies to her whole income, not the extra ₹1 lakh. + About ₹20,800 || Extra income is taxed at the marginal rate (assuming it stays within the same slab). - About ₹30,000 || That would be the top rate plus cess, which is not her slab. ? Why do health insurers charge premiums higher than the expected claim, and why is buying still sensible? + The premium covers claims plus costs and profit; buying makes sense because it removes the risk of a ruinous loss you could not absorb || Insurance trades a small certain cost for protection against a large uncertain one. - Premiums are higher because insurers expect everyone to claim; buying makes sense because you will get more back || Most policyholders claim less than they pay, on average. - They are not higher; competition keeps premiums equal to expected claims || Insurers have costs and need profit, so premiums exceed expected claims.

Calculation

? A portfolio grew from ₹4,00,000 to ₹7,00,000 in 6 years. What was its CAGR, in % to two decimals? = (7 ÷ 4)1/6 − 1 = 1.750.1667 − 1 = 9.78%. Not 12.5% (75% ÷ 6), which ignores compounding. ? You need ₹15 lakh in 10 years and can earn 8% a year. How much must you set aside today, in ₹ lakh to two decimals? = 15 ÷ 1.0810 = 15 ÷ 2.1589 = ₹6.95 lakh. ? A fund returned 11% in a year of 6% inflation. What was its real return, in % to two decimals? = 1.11 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = 4.72%, not 5%. ? A ₹30 lakh home loan at 9% for 15 years has an EMI of ₹30,428. What percentage of the first EMI is interest (nearest whole %)? = Month-1 interest = 30,00,000 × 0.09 ÷ 12 = ₹22,500. Share = 22,500 ÷ 30,428 = 74%. ? A household's essentials are ₹80,000 a month. After a job loss, rental income of ₹20,000 a month continues. Its stable buffer is ₹2.7 lakh. What is its runway in months? = Net outflow = 80,000 − 20,000 = ₹60,000. Runway = 2,70,000 ÷ 60,000 = 4.5 months.

Interpretation and critique

? A message says: "Earn 2.5% monthly, guaranteed, capital protected. Limited slots for trusted members." What is the strongest single reason to decline? - The slots are limited || Scarcity is a pressure tactic, but not the core problem. + A guaranteed 34% a year (2.5% monthly compounded) with no risk does not exist in legitimate investing; the guarantee itself is the warning sign || Annualise and compare with the safe rate: the claim fails the guarantee test. - The return is too low to be worth it || 34% a year guaranteed would be extraordinary, not low. ? A policy illustration shows: pay ₹50,000 a year for 15 years; receive ₹10 lakh at the end of year 20, with ₹5 lakh of life cover. Which is the most useful first step in evaluating it? + Compute the internal rate of return on the premiums and compare it, and the cover, with term insurance plus a separate investment || Separate the insurance decision from the investment decision and compare each with its best alternative. - Check that the maturity amount is larger than the total premiums || ₹10 lakh exceeds ₹7.5 lakh of premiums, but that ignores 20 years of time value and inflation. - Choose it if the insurer is large and well known || Reputation matters for claims, not for whether the product is a good investment. = The internal rate of return here is only about 2.2% a year, well below inflation, and ₹5 lakh of life cover is small for most earners with dependants. ? A retirement calculator says you need ₹5 crore at 60. You are 30. Which interpretation is most accurate? - ₹5 crore of today's purchasing power || The figure is in future rupees; at 5% inflation it is worth about ₹1.16 crore today. + It is a future-rupee figure built on assumptions about returns, inflation and lifespan; the useful outputs are the monthly saving it implies and how sensitive that is to the assumptions || Treat any single figure as the centre of a wide range and look at what drives it. - It is a precise target you must hit exactly || Small changes in assumptions move it by crores.

Application

? Tanvi is a freelance video editor. Her income varies from ₹40,000 to ₹2 lakh a month; her essentials are ₹50,000. She has ₹1 lakh saved, a ₹60,000 card balance, and wants to start a SIP. What should come first? - Start the SIP now; time in the market matters most || Investing while carrying a card balance at over 40% a year, with a thin buffer and irregular income, puts the plan at risk. + Clear the card, then build a larger-than-usual emergency fund (about 9 months of essentials, given irregular income), budgeting from her low months; start the SIP once those are in place || Buffer and expensive debt first; her variable income needs a longer runway. - Take a personal loan to clear the card and invest the ₹1 lakh || That swaps one debt for another and leaves her with no buffer. ? The Mehtas have a ₹6 lakh emergency fund (6 months of essentials), no other debt, and a home loan at 8.3% floating. They receive a ₹5 lakh bonus. They are considering prepaying the loan or investing for their child's education in 14 years. Which reasoning is sound? - Always prepay, because being debt-free is always best || Prepayment earns a certain 8.3% (before any tax effects), but it is not always superior; the long horizon of the education goal matters. - Always invest, because markets beat 8.3% || Not guaranteed; the comparison must include risk. + Both are reasonable: prepaying gives a certain 8.3% return and there is no prepayment charge on their floating-rate loan; investing for a 14-year goal may earn more but with risk; the choice depends on their risk tolerance and whether the education goal is on track || With the buffer in place and no expensive debt, this is a genuine trade-off, not a rule.

Arjun, 32, takes home ₹1.4 lakh a month and spends ₹1.25 lakh, including a ₹38,000 car-loan EMI and ₹6,000 of card interest. He has ₹70,000 in savings, ₹5 lakh in equity funds, no health insurance beyond his employer's ₹3 lakh group cover, and a spouse who is not working. He asks whether he should buy a larger flat with a new loan.

In 150 to 250 words: diagnose his cash flow, balance sheet, buffers and protection; state what he should do before considering the flat, and why.

{{i:eye}}After writingCompare with a model answerShow

Cash flow: savings rate about 10.7% (₹15,000 of ₹1.4 lakh), with ₹6,000 a month lost to card interest; EMIs are about 27% of take-home before any new loan.

Balance sheet and buffers: ₹70,000 is about half a month of spending. His ₹5 lakh is in volatile assets, so his stable runway is very short for a single-earner household with a dependant.

Protection: no personal health cover and, as the sole earner, likely no adequate term cover; his family's biggest risks are uninsured.

Before any flat: clear the card (a near-certain 40%+ return), build 6 to 9 months of essentials in stable form, buy personal health cover and term life cover sized to his family's needs, and only then test whether a larger EMI fits comfortably inside free cash flow with a 2-point rate-rise stress test. A larger loan now would raise his fixed costs and leverage while his buffers and protection are weakest.
