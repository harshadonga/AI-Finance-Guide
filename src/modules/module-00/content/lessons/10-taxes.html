Taxes: The After-Tax View

Big ideaJudge every financial decision after tax. What matters is the rate on your next rupee, not your average rate, and tax paid every year compounds into a large drag.

Why after taxHow slabs workCurrent rulesSlab visualiserAfter-tax returnsTax dragPractice

Why this lesson is deliberately narrow

Tax rules are long, change with almost every budget, and differ by type of income. Level 23 is a living module that covers them properly and is refreshed each time it is studied. This lesson teaches only what you need now: the handful of ideas that let you compare choices after tax, and that stay true however the rates change.

{{i:lightbulb}} Intuition

Picture income tax slabs as a row of buckets with rising tax rates. Your income fills the buckets in order: the first bucket, taxed at 0%, fills first; overflow goes into the 5% bucket, then the 10% bucket, and so on. Only the rupees that land in a bucket pay that bucket's rate. Earning more never makes the rupees already in lower buckets pay more.

? Meera's raise moves her taxable income from ₹11.5 lakh to ₹14.25 lakh, crossing into the 15% slab. Tax rises from nil to ₹97,500 (including cess), an average of 6.5% of her ₹15 lakh gross, while her marginal rate is 15.6%. At ₹11.5 lakh of taxable income she paid nothing because of the rebate; at ₹14.25 lakh the rebate no longer applies, which is why the jump looks large. The rates that matter for decisions are marginal, and they apply to extra income only.

Marginal and average rates

Your [[marginal-tax-rate|marginal tax rate]] is the rate on your next rupee of income. Your [[average-tax-rate|average tax rate]] is total tax divided by total income. They answer different questions. The average rate describes your overall burden. The marginal rate decides whether an extra piece of work, a deduction, or a taxable investment is worth it, because those change your income at the margin.

The current rules, in brief

{{i:seal-check}} Current rules · Individuals, new regime

{{tag:curr:29 Sep 2026}}
  • New law. The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961 from 1 April 2026. It uses the single term tax year in place of "previous year" and "assessment year". Slab rates for tax year 2026-27 are unchanged from 2025-26.
  • Default regime. The new tax regime is the default for individuals; the old regime, with its deductions and exemptions, can still be chosen.
  • Slabs (taxable income): up to ₹4 lakh nil · ₹4 to 8 lakh 5% · ₹8 to 12 lakh 10% · ₹12 to 16 lakh 15% · ₹16 to 20 lakh 20% · ₹20 to 24 lakh 25% · above ₹24 lakh 30%.
  • Rebate. A rebate makes tax nil when taxable income does not exceed ₹12 lakh. With the ₹75,000 standard deduction for salaried taxpayers, that is a gross salary of ₹12.75 lakh. Just above the limit, marginal relief caps the tax at the amount by which income exceeds ₹12 lakh.
  • Cess. A 4% health and education cess applies to the tax. Surcharges apply at higher incomes (above ₹50 lakh) and are outside this lesson.

{{i:warning}} Caution · The one place a raise barely helps

Between a salary of ₹12.75 lakh and about ₹13.46 lakh, marginal relief means almost every extra rupee of income goes in tax (about 104% including cess, applied only to the excess). Take-home still does not fall, but it hardly rises. Above about ₹13.46 lakh, the normal slab calculation takes over and the marginal rate drops to 15.6%. After-tax returns

Interest from bank deposits is added to your income and taxed at your slab rate. So the return you keep from a fixed deposit depends on your marginal rate. The [[after-tax-return|after-tax return]] on a fully taxed return is simple:

{{i:function}} Formula · After-tax and after-tax real return

rafter tax=r×(1−t)rreal, after tax=1+r(1−t)1+π−1
r
Pre-tax return, taxed each year.
t
Marginal tax rate that applies to that return, including cess.
π
Inflation (Lesson 0.5).

Worked example. A 7% FD. At Meera's 15.6% marginal rate: 7% × 0.844 = 5.91% after tax, and (1.0591 ÷ 1.05) − 1 = 0.87% real. At a 31.2% marginal rate (30% slab plus cess): 4.82% after tax, and −0.18% real. The same deposit loses purchasing power for a higher earner.

Limitations. This applies to returns taxed every year at slab rates, such as deposit interest. Other investments are taxed differently (for example, gains taxed only when you sell, or at special rates). Those rules are in Level 23.

This finishes a thread from Lesson 0.6: the goal planner used pre-tax returns. For Meera's car goal, a 6.5% deposit taxed at 15.6% earns 5.49% after tax, so the monthly saving needed is slightly higher than the planner showed. For short goals the difference is small; for long goals it compounds.

Tax drag: why timing of tax matters

[[tax-drag|Tax drag]] is the reduction in long-run wealth caused by paying tax on returns. It depends not only on the rate but on when tax is paid. Tax taken every year shrinks the amount left to compound. Tax paid once at the end lets the whole amount compound first.

{{i:calculator}} Worked example · Same rate, different timing

{{tag:hyp}}

₹10 lakh earning 10% a year for 20 years, with a 20% tax rate on returns:

  • No tax: ₹67.3 lakh.
  • Tax paid once at the end on the total gain: ₹10 lakh + (₹57.3 lakh × 0.8) = ₹55.8 lakh.
  • Tax paid every year: the money compounds at 8% instead of 10% = ₹46.6 lakh.

Interpretation. Same tax rate, ₹9.2 lakh difference, purely from timing. This is why the tax treatment of an investment is part of its return, and why Level 23 matters for anyone investing for decades. The rates here are illustrative, not the rates on any specific product.

Common misconceptions

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“A raise can push me into a higher bracket and reduce my take-home.”

Why this failsSlab rates apply only to income within each slab, so more income always leaves more after tax. The one narrow exception is the marginal-relief band just above the rebate limit, where take-home barely rises but still does not fall.

Better questionWhat is my marginal rate on this extra income?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“It saves tax, so it must be a good investment.”

Why this failsA tax saving is one part of the return. A product with a tax benefit and a poor underlying return, a long lock-in or high costs can still be worse than a taxable alternative. Under the default new regime, many older deductions do not apply at all.

Better questionWhat is the after-tax return, including the tax benefit, compared with the best alternative?

Where this breaks

  • Rules change. Slabs, rebates and deductions are revised in budgets. Every figure here carries a verification date for that reason.
  • Different income, different rules. Capital gains, dividends and some other income are taxed at special rates or in special ways; the slab model here applies to salary and deposit interest.
  • Old vs new regime. The choice depends on deductions you can claim; comparing them is Level 23 work.
  • Surcharge, TDS and filing. Deliberately excluded here.

A: (a) True: taxable income is ₹11.75 lakh after the ₹75,000 standard deduction, within the ₹12 lakh rebate limit. (b) False: only the slice from ₹16 to 20 lakh is taxed at 20%. (c) True. # B Compute the tax (with cess) on a salary of ₹20 lakh gross under the simplified model, and the average and marginal rates. A: Taxable ₹19.25 lakh. Slabs: 20,000 + 40,000 + 60,000 (15% of ₹4 lakh) + 65,000 (20% of ₹3.25 lakh) = ₹1,85,000; with 4% cess ₹1,92,400. Average = 9.6% of gross; marginal = 20.8%. # C Meera is offered ₹60,000 for a weekend consulting project. Roughly how much does she keep, and what else should she consider? A: At her 15.6% marginal rate, tax ≈ ₹9,360, leaving about ₹50,640. She should also consider whether her employment contract allows outside work and any professional costs. The marginal rate, not the 6.5% average, is the right one. # D For a 20-year goal, compare a 7% investment taxed every year at 31.2% with a 7% investment taxed once at the end at 20% on the total gain, for ₹5 lakh. A: Annual taxation: 7% × 0.688 = 4.82%; ₹5 lakh × 1.048220 ≈ ₹12.81 lakh. Deferred: ₹5 lakh × 1.0720 = ₹19.35 lakh; gain ₹14.35 lakh taxed at 20% leaves ₹16.48 lakh. Deferral and the lower rate together add about ₹3.7 lakh. (Illustrative rates; actual treatment depends on the product, Level 23.) # E A friend argues that because the rebate makes income up to ₹12 lakh tax-free, "taxes don't matter for middle-class investors." Evaluate this. A: The rebate zeroes tax on salary within the limit, but investment income adds to total income and can push it above the limit, and many investors earn more than ₹12 lakh. Even at lower incomes, the principles (marginal rates, after-tax returns, timing of tax) decide which investments are better. Taxes matter to almost everyone through their effect on long-run compounding.
