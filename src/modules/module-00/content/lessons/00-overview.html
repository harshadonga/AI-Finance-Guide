Personal Finance & Wealth Foundations

Understand the financial system of one household, your own, before trying to optimise any investment.

The big idea

If you remember one idea from this moduleYour finances are a system of stocks and flows. Time turns flows into stocks through compounding. Inflation, taxes and expensive debt drain that conversion. Liquidity and insurance protect it from shocks, and fraud attacks it directly.

The module as one system

Every lesson is one part of the same machine. Flows are measured per month. Stocks are measured on a date. Forces act on both over time. Select any part to go to its lesson.

By the end you should be able to

  • {{i:check}} explain the difference between income and wealth, and why human capital is usually a young person's largest asset;
  • {{i:check}} build your own monthly cash-flow statement and balance sheet, and compute savings rate, net worth, liquid net worth and leverage;
  • {{i:check}} calculate future value, present value, CAGR and effective annual rates, and explain why time matters more than rate;
  • {{i:check}} convert nominal returns into real returns correctly, and explain what CPI measures;
  • {{i:check}} size a financial goal in future rupees and work out the monthly saving it needs;
  • {{i:check}} size an emergency fund from your own circumstances and choose where to keep it;
  • {{i:check}} read a loan the way a lender does: EMI, amortisation, flat vs reducing rates, prepayment and refinancing;
  • {{i:check}} decide what to insure, estimate how much cover you need, and separate insurance from investment;
  • {{i:check}} judge a decision after tax using the marginal rate;
  • {{i:check}} recognise the mechanics and warning signs of Ponzi schemes, impersonation and phishing, and know how to verify and report.

Questions this module answers

  1. Why do some high earners have no wealth?
  2. Is a 7% fixed deposit a good return?
  3. How can a 20-year loan cost more in interest than the amount borrowed?
  4. Why is it rational to buy insurance that loses money on average?
  5. Why must every "guaranteed 3% a month" scheme eventually collapse?

The household we will follow

{{i:user}} Running example

{{tag:hyp}}

Meera Iyer, 29, is a product analyst in Pune. Her gross salary is ₹15 lakh a year and about ₹1.10 lakh a month reaches her bank account. She rents a flat, has a credit-card balance she has not cleared, an education loan, a small fixed deposit, some mutual funds and her provident fund. She has no dependants today.

Where a lesson needs dependants, a home loan or life cover, we use a second household: the Nairs, both 38, two incomes, one child and a home loan.

Both households are invented. Their numbers are chosen to be realistic for urban India in 2026, not to describe any real person.

How to read this module

Each lesson runs in the same order: why the idea exists, an intuition, a prediction for you to make, a worked example, an interactive explorer, the formal mathematics, a realistic application, where the idea breaks, and practice. You can skip ahead; the lesson list shows a tick when you have answered a lesson's knowledge checks.
