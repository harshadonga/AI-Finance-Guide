Debt

Big ideaDebt is compounding working against you. It is a useful tool when it buys an asset or capability worth more than its cost, and a trap when it funds consumption at high rates.

Why people borrow, and when it makes sense

Borrowing moves spending from the future to the present. That is genuinely valuable when the thing bought produces value over time: a home you would otherwise rent, an education that raises your earnings, a vehicle you need for work. It is costly when it pays for consumption that is gone before the loan is. The interest is the price of having it now.

{{i:lightbulb}} Intuition

A loan is renting money. The interest rate is the rent. Rent is worth paying for something that earns or saves you more than it costs. Paying 42% a year to rent money for a phone upgrade is renting at a price almost nothing you buy can justify.

Lesson 0.4 showed compounding working for you. Debt is the same mathematics facing the other way: an unpaid balance grows at the loan's rate, and every rupee of interest paid is a rupee that cannot compound in your favour.

Secured and unsecured debt

[[secured-debt|Secured debt]] is backed by [[collateral|collateral]]: an asset the lender can take and sell if you do not repay. Home loans (secured by the house), car loans and gold loans are secured. Because the lender's risk is lower, rates are lower. [[unsecured-debt|Unsecured debt]] has no collateral: personal loans, credit-card balances, most app-based loans. The lender relies only on your promise and your credit record, so it charges more.

The EMI

An [[emi|EMI]] (equated monthly instalment) is a fixed monthly payment that covers the month's interest and repays some principal, set so that the loan reaches exactly zero at the end. It is an annuity (Lesson 0.4): the lender hands you a lump sum today in exchange for a series of equal payments whose present value, at the loan rate, equals that lump sum.

{{i:function}} Formula · EMI

EMI=P×i×(1+i)n(1+i)n−1
P
Principal borrowed, in ₹.
i
Monthly interest rate = annual rate ÷ 12, by lending convention (8.5% a year → 0.7083% a month).
n
Number of monthly instalments (20 years = 240).

Where it comes from. Set the present value of n payments of EMI equal to P: P = EMI × [1 − (1 + i)−n] ÷ i. Solve for EMI.

Worked example. P = ₹50,00,000, i = 0.085 ÷ 12, n = 240: EMI = ₹43,391.

Sensitivity. A longer tenure lowers the EMI but raises total interest sharply: at 30 years, the EMI falls to ₹38,446 but total interest rises to ₹88.4 lakh. A 1-point higher rate (9.5%) raises the 20-year EMI to ₹46,607.

Convention note. Lenders divide the annual rate by 12 rather than using the effective monthly rate of Lesson 0.4. The true effective annual cost of "8.5% a year" charged monthly is (1 + 0.085/12)12 − 1 = 8.84%.

Amortisation and reducing balance

[[amortisation|Amortisation]] is the gradual repayment of a loan through scheduled payments. Under [[reducing-balance|reducing-balance]] interest, each month's interest is charged only on what is still owed. Early on the balance is large, so interest takes most of the EMI. As the balance shrinks, interest falls and more of each (unchanged) EMI repays principal. The split reverses over the life of the loan.

Interestm=Bm−1×iPrincipalm=EMI−InterestmBm=Bm−1−Principalm
Bm
Outstanding balance after month m, in ₹. B0 = P.

Flat rate vs reducing balance: the personal-loan trap

Some lenders, particularly for consumer durables, two-wheelers and some personal loans, quote a [[flat-rate|flat rate]]: interest calculated on the original amount for the whole term, even though you repay part of it every month. A flat rate looks roughly half as expensive as it really is.

{{i:calculator}} Worked example · "Only 10% flat"

{{tag:hyp}}

A ₹3 lakh personal loan for 3 years at "10% flat". Interest = 3,00,000 × 10% × 3 = ₹90,000. EMI = (3,00,000 + 90,000) ÷ 36 = ₹10,833.

The reducing-balance rate that produces the same EMI is 17.9% a year. You are paying interest on money you have already repaid.

Interpretation. Always ask for the reducing-balance rate (or the annual percentage rate, including fees) before comparing loans. Two quotes of "10%" can differ by 8 percentage points.

Credit cards: free if cleared, ruinous if not

A credit card is [[revolving-credit|revolving credit]]: you can borrow up to a limit, repay, and borrow again. Used well it is a free payment tool: pay the full statement balance by the due date and there is usually no interest at all. Used as a loan it is the most expensive mainstream borrowing available, typically around 3% to 3.75% a month on revolving balances (check your card's terms), which compounds to over 40% a year.

Two features make it dangerous. First, once you carry any balance forward, the interest-free period on new purchases is usually suspended, so new spending starts accruing interest immediately. Second, the [[minimum-due|minimum amount due]] is set so that the balance does shrink, but only slowly, which makes paying the minimum feel responsible while it keeps you in debt for years.

{{i:seal-check}} Current rules · RBI Master Direction on credit cards

{{tag:curr:29 Sep 2026}}
  • The minimum amount due must be set so that there is no negative amortisation: paying it must not let the balance grow.
  • The card's terms must state that the interest-free period is suspended if any balance from the previous bill is outstanding.
  • Late-payment charges may be levied only on the amount outstanding after the due date, not on the total amount due, and an account can be reported as past due or charged late fees only when it has been past due for more than three days.
  • Interest may be charged only on the outstanding amount, adjusted for payments, refunds and reversed transactions.

Home loans and floating rates

Most Indian home loans carry a floating rate linked to an external benchmark, so the rate moves when the benchmark moves (how the RBI's policy rate feeds through is Lesson 6.8). When rates change mid-loan, the lender either changes the EMI or changes the remaining tenure.

{{i:calculator}} Worked example · A rate rise in year 3

{{tag:hyp}}

After three years of EMIs, the Nairs owe ₹46.74 lakh with 204 months left. Their rate rises from 8.5% to 9.5%.

  • Keep the tenure: the EMI rises from ₹43,391 to ₹46,265.
  • Keep the EMI: the loan runs 39 months longer, over three extra years of payments.

Interpretation. Tenure extension feels painless because nothing changes this month, but it quietly adds years of interest. Borrowers who can afford it are usually better off accepting the higher EMI. Lenders are required to inform borrowers of such changes; ask which option has been applied to your loan.

A useful stress test before borrowing: could you still pay the EMI if the rate rose two percentage points? For the Nairs' original loan, 10.5% would mean an EMI of about ₹49,900.

Refinancing

[[refinancing|Refinancing]] replaces an existing loan with a new one on better terms, usually by moving the balance to another lender (a balance transfer) or renegotiating with your current lender. It is worth doing when the interest saved exceeds the cost of switching.

{{i:function}} Formula · Refinancing break-even

Break-even months=Switching costEMIold−EMInew
Switching cost
Processing fees, legal and valuation charges, stamp duty where applicable, in ₹.
EMIold − EMInew
Monthly saving at the same remaining tenure, in ₹.

Worked example. ₹40 lakh outstanding, 17 years (204 months) left, rate falls from 9.25% to 8.40%, switching cost ₹15,000. EMI falls from ₹38,969 to ₹36,890, a saving of ₹2,080 a month. Break-even ≈ 7 months; total saving over the remaining term ≈ ₹4.1 lakh after costs.

Limitations. Ignores differences in service, reset terms and the time spent switching. Ask your current lender first: many will reprice to keep you.

Prepayment: shorten, lower, or invest instead?

[[prepayment|Prepaying]] means repaying principal ahead of schedule. Every prepaid rupee earns a guaranteed return equal to the loan's interest rate, because it stops that rupee from being charged interest.

{{i:calculator}} Worked example · ₹5 lakh after year 3

{{tag:hyp}}
  • Shorten the loan (keep EMI): the loan ends in month 199 instead of 240; total interest ₹40.9 lakh instead of ₹54.1 lakh. Saving ≈ ₹13.2 lakh.
  • Lower the EMI (keep tenure): the EMI falls to ₹38,750; total interest ₹49.7 lakh. Saving ≈ ₹4.5 lakh, plus ₹4,640 a month of breathing room.

Interpretation. Shortening saves far more interest, because the whole EMI keeps attacking a smaller balance. Lowering the EMI makes sense when cash flow is tight or income is uncertain: it improves resilience at the cost of interest.

Prepay or invest?

The comparison is between the loan's rate after tax and the investment's expected return after tax, with two adjustments. The prepayment's return is certain; the investment's is not. And prepaid money is gone: you cannot get it back in an emergency. A reasonable order: build the emergency fund first (Lesson 0.7), then clear any debt above roughly 12% to 15% (card balances, personal loans) as a near-certain high return, then weigh prepaying low-rate secured debt against long-term investing in light of your goals and risk tolerance. There is no single right answer for the last step; there is a clear right answer for the 42% card.

{{i:seal-check}} Current rule · Prepayment charges

{{tag:curr:29 Sep 2026}}

Under the RBI's Pre-payment Charges on Loans Directions, 2025, for loans sanctioned or renewed on or after 1 January 2026, regulated lenders (commercial banks other than payments banks, co-operative banks, NBFCs and all-India financial institutions) may not levy prepayment charges on floating-rate loans to individuals for non-business purposes, with or without co-borrowers. Fixed-rate loans and business loans follow different rules; check your loan agreement.

Several debts: avalanche or snowball

With several debts and spare cash, the avalanche method pays the highest-rate debt first while paying minimums on the rest; it minimises total interest. The snowball method pays the smallest balance first for a quick sense of progress; it costs more in interest but some people find it easier to stick with. Mathematically, avalanche wins. Behaviourally, the best method is the one you will actually follow, which is a first glimpse of Lesson 9's theme.

Understand the EMI as a present-value identity by building the schedule month by month, and verify it with independent checks.

{{i:database}} Data

The Nairs' loan: ₹50,00,000, 8.5% a year, 240 months. {{tag:hyp}}

{{i:table}} Build it in a spreadsheet

  1. Put the inputs in cells: B1 = 5000000, B2 = 0.085, B3 = 240.
  2. EMI in B4: =PMT(B2/12, B3, -B1). The minus sign is the spreadsheet's convention that money received is positive and money paid is negative.
  3. Columns from row 7: Month, Opening balance, Interest = opening × $B$2/12, Principal = $B$4 − interest, Closing balance = opening − principal. Each row's opening balance is the previous row's closing balance.
  4. Fill down to month 240.

{{i:check-circle}} Failure checks

  • Closing balance in month 240 is zero (within a rupee).
  • Sum of the principal column equals ₹50,00,000.
  • Month-1 interest equals P × i = ₹35,417.
  • Common errors: using the annual rate as monthly (EMI far too high), forgetting the minus sign in PMT (negative EMI), rounding each row so the final balance drifts from zero.

{{i:lightbulb}} Interpretation

Plot interest and principal per year. The crossing point, where principal first exceeds interest, arrives in year 13. Before it, you are mostly paying rent on money; after it, mostly buying back your house.

Add a column for an extra payment and put ₹5,00,000 in month 36. Keep the EMI fixed and let the balance hit zero early: you should reproduce the loan ending in month 199 and interest of about ₹40.9 lakh. Then recompute the EMI on the remaining balance for 204 months and confirm ₹38,750.

{{i:code}} Optional preview · The same schedule in Python (Level 13 teaches this properly) {{i:caret-right}}

Not required for this module. Shown so you can see that code expresses the same three-line loop.

principal, annual_rate, months = 5_000_000, 0.085, 240
i = annual_rate / 12
emi = principal * i * (1 + i) ** months / ((1 + i) ** months - 1)

balance, total_interest = principal, 0.0
for m in range(1, months + 1):
    interest = balance * i            # interest on what is still owed
    repaid = emi - interest           # the rest reduces the loan
    balance -= repaid
    total_interest += interest

print(f"EMI: {emi:,.0f}")                       # 43,391
print(f"Total interest: {total_interest:,.0f}") # 5,413,879
print(f"Final balance: {balance:,.2f}")         # ~0.00  (check)

Common misconceptions

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“A lower EMI means a cheaper loan.”

Why this failsEMIs fall when tenure rises, and total interest rises much faster. A 30-year loan has an EMI 11% lower than a 20-year loan at 8.5%, with 63% more interest.

Better questionWhat is the total interest, and what is the effective annual rate including fees?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“No-cost EMI means no cost.”

Why this fails"No-cost" EMI offers typically work by giving a discount equal to the interest, so the interest is still charged by the lender; there may be processing fees, GST on the interest component, and loss of a cash discount you could otherwise have negotiated.

Better questionWhat is the lowest price I could pay today in cash, and what is the total I will pay through the EMI?

{{i:prohibit}} Common misconception

“All debt is bad, so I should never borrow.”

Why this failsA home loan instead of rent, or an education loan that raises earnings, can improve a household's long-run position. The question is always the rate, the purpose, and whether the payments fit comfortably inside free cash flow with a buffer.

Better questionDoes this debt buy something worth more than its cost, and can I carry it through a bad year?

Where this breaks

  • Rates are not fixed. Floating-rate schedules change whenever the benchmark changes; any projection beyond a year or two is illustrative.
  • Affordability heuristics are rough. A common rule of thumb is to keep total EMIs below about 40% to 50% of take-home. It ignores dependants, job security and other commitments. It is a ceiling, not a target.
  • Hidden costs. Processing fees, insurance bundled with loans, and late-payment charges raise the effective cost above the quoted rate.
  • Co-signing is borrowing. Guaranteeing someone else's loan makes you liable for it (a contingent liability, Lesson 0.3).

A: Partly reasonable: the longer tenure gives flexibility and a lower mandatory payment, and with no prepayment charges on floating-rate individual loans, prepaying is possible.
